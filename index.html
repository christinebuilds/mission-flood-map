Posts are anonymous. Notes and photos are checked automatically before they appear publicly; photos with faces or license plates are not shown. For danger to life call 9-1-1. This does not notify the city; use SF311 for that.

What each layer means

  • City flood zone (purple): the SFPUC's official 100-year storm flood risk zone, from DataSF. It models a very rare storm and covers storm runoff only, not bay or ocean flooding. Use it to see which blocks sit low.
  • Past storms (dashed shading): intersections and streets that news reports say flooded before. These are estimates of where water tends to collect, not a record of this week. Red is a repeat hotspot; orange was reported once or twice.
  • 311 reports (solid dots): flooding-related cases people filed with SF311 in the window you pick. Blue is flooding, teal is a storm drain needing maintenance, purple is a sewage backup. Pale dots are closed cases. The public dataset updates nightly, around 6 am (DataSF), so today's reports appear the next morning.
  • Storm drains: city drain locations from DataSF. Pink means check now (a neighbor flagged it, or an open 311 flooding or drain case is within 60 m). Amber means check first (inside the city flood zone, or in or next to a past-flood area). Green was cleared by a neighbor in the last 14 days. These rankings are this map's own rule of thumb, not a city assessment. Tap a drain to mark it cleared or flag it, with a photo. To adopt one officially, go to Adopt a Drain SF.
  • Neighbor reports (cyan): "water here" posts from people using this map in the last 48 hours. Unverified.
  • Wildlife seen (white-ringed dots): plants, birds, insects, fungi and other wildlife that people photographed and posted to iNaturalist in the last 30 days. Tap a dot for the photo, the name and a short description. Zoom in to a neighborhood to load them. Only sightings whose photographers allow reuse are shown, with credit.
  • Street trees (green): the city's street tree list from DataSF. Dark green marks California native species, which feed far more insects and birds. Zoom in to street level to see them, and tap one for a photo of the species. Trees and their leaves are also why drains clog: the leafiest blocks are the ones to check before rain.
  • Chips above the map: National Weather Service alerts and rain chance, and the next high tide from NOAA's San Francisco gauge. Heavy rain near high tide drains slowest.
  • Shapes are drawn by hand from the intersections articles name, so edges are accurate to a block or two. News coverage is uneven across neighborhoods, so a street with no shading is not necessarily safe.

How to read this for 1 to 2 inches of rain

  • The Rintaro flood came on a day with 5.46 inches, the second-wettest day on record in San Francisco (SF Chronicle). That is roughly three to five times your rainfall.
  • Rain per hour matters more than the total. In December 2019, much of the rain came down in about an hour (ABC7).
  • Smaller storms have mostly meant ponding. After a storm that gave many areas an inch or more, public works dealt with about 15 flooded areas, mainly intersections (NBC Bay Area).
  • My reading, not a forecast: steady rain over many hours means ponding in the red areas. A burst of an inch or more in an hour, or clogged drains, is when the New Year's Eve pattern repeats.

For your own block, check the SFPUC flood map. To report flooding, use SF311 or call 3-1-1. Browse all 311 cases in the city's 311 explorer.
